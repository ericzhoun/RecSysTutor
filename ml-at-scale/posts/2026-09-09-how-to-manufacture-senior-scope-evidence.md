# How to manufacture senior-scope evidence before anyone gives you the title

*Or: the chicken-and-egg buried inside every senior promo*

[![](../assets/db80e0986af49867.webp)](../assets/db80e0986af49867.webp)

Nobody is going to hand you senior scope. So how do you get it?

Here is the loop:

Promotion to senior requires evidence that you already operated at senior scope. Scope is assigned by your manager and the org. And the org does not assign senior scope to someone who is not senior yet, because that is a bet nobody is incentivized to place.

You need the scope to earn the level, and you need the level to get the scope.

Most people never see the loop at all :D. They assume promo is a function of working harder on the scope they already have.

**It is not.**

Working harder on your current scope produces a very strong version of your current level, which is exactly the record the committee uses to keep you where you are.

Over the last year I built a senior-scope evidence trail as an L4. No title change. No headcount. No manager redrawing my scope.

Nobody handed me a single thing. Here is the method, in five parts:

  1. Why “do great work and get noticed” is a trap.

  2. Why “ask for bigger scope” is also a trap.

  3. The actual move, which is neither waiting nor asking.

  4. How to build the evidence trail

  5. The failure modes, including the one that will make your own team resent you.

Let me clear the two dead ends first, because most people are stuck in one of them.

**Bad answer #1: Wait to be noticed.**

Do excellent work. Assume the committee will see the quality and reward it.
They will. They will reward it correctly, as excellent work at your current level. Quality is not scope. A committee looking for senior evidence does not find it in a flawless stack of mid-level deliverables.
It finds a very reliable mid-level engineer, and it writes exactly that.

**Bad answer #2: Ask for the scope.**

Go to your manager. Request a bigger project, a headcount, a lead role. Two outcomes, both bad. Either you hear “not yet, keep growing,” which quietly resets your clock. Or you get granted something, and granted scope reads in a packet as “was given a chance,” not “created leverage the org came to depend on.”

Assigned scope is a gift, and a gift does not demonstrate the one thing senior is supposed to demonstrate: that you generate scope rather than receive it.

So if you cannot wait for it, and you cannot ask for it, what is left?

There is a third move. It is the one that actually worked for me, and it is the reason I am walking into calibration this year with a packet that argues itself. It has nothing to do with working harder, and nothing to do with lobbying.

## The move: become the point of contact in a gap nobody owns

Orgs are not static. Reorgs happen. Reqs open. Teams get spun up without a lead. People leave. Charters split. Every one of these events creates scope that is temporarily ownerless.
Not ownerless because it is unimportant. Ownerless because the assignment has not caught up to the org chart yet.

That gap is the only place a mid-level engineer can operate at senior scope without asking permission, because there is no incumbent to displace and no favor to request. You do not take the scope. You become the person the scope routes through while nobody is looking.

Here is how it played out for me.

About six months ago a chunk of what I owned got carved out into a new pod.
New charter, its own ICs, roadmap commitments, and an open TL req that is still being recruited for.

The territorial move would have been to fight it in the reorg doc and CC my skip.

The clean move would have been to hand it off in a doc, wish them luck, and walk away.

I did neither.

I used the wind-down quarter my manager gave me to write the design docs and handoff materials the new pod would need. Then I stayed close as the connective tissue: feeding them context, direction, and the history only I had, because the role meant to do that did not exist yet.

Notice what that actually is. Not a title. Not a promotion. Not something anyone above me announced.

It is a set of edges in the org graph that quietly route through a mid-level engineer in a different pod, in a gap that exists only because the lead hire has not closed. That is senior scope. I just did not wait for anyone to label it.

## The playbook: become the point of contact WITHOUT overclaiming

  1. **Spot the gap early.** The clearest signal is an open lead req on a team that still has to ship. Every open lead role is a window. So is a reorg, a departure, a charter split. Learn to read the org chart for the seams.

  2. **Lead with artifacts, not authority.** Write the design doc the gap needs. Write the handoff. Be the person whose document everyone opens. A document is influence that does not require a title, and it does not ask anyone’s permission to exist.

  3. **Be the lowest-friction path to context.** When the ICs start pinging you before they ping each other, that is the graph rewiring itself around you. You get there by being reliably useful and fast, not by asserting anything.

  4. **Never say the quiet part.** Do not call yourself the lead. Do not say you run anything. The move works because it is invisible. The moment you narrate it, it becomes a flex, and a flex invites someone to correct the record.

## The evidence trail: legible without being a claim

The single cleanest senior narrative you can write is “I was the connective tissue for the pod for N months while the lead role was open.” That sentence is just “I did the job before I had the title,” and it is the most credible form of senior evidence there is, because it is not a request. It is a record.

Capture the artifacts as you go. The design docs with your name on them. The handoff materials. The launches that trace back to work you seeded. A packet built on artifacts is a packet the committee cannot wave away.

And get other people to say it for you. The strongest line in your packet is not you claiming influence. It is a peer, or one of the new pod’s ICs, writing on their own that they routed decisions through you. Evidence you built yourself, corroborated by other people, is not manufactured anymore. It is just true.

## Failure modes

  1. **The clock is real.** When the lead hire lands, the window closes. Convert the influence into documented evidence before that happens, or it evaporates with the next reorg.

  2. **Hand off clean.** When the real lead arrives, step back gracefully and do not cling. Clinging turns a great story (”built the bridge, handed it off”) into a bad one (”could not let go”). What you keep after the handoff is the relationships, not the control.

  3. **Do not threaten a partial owner.** If anyone owns even a slice of the gap, bring them in rather than around. This is not about being territorial, and the whole difference is whether people are glad you are in the gap or wary of it.

  4. **Your own team is watching.** If any of this reads as “I am secretly running another pod,” it travels back badly. Keep the framing quiet and generous everywhere, including in a newsletter your colleagues read. ;)

## The read

Senior is not a reward for doing your current job well. It is evidence that you operate at the next level before anyone pays you to. Nobody assigns that evidence.
The org is too risk-averse to hand it out and too busy to notice when you build it yourself.

The gaps are always there: reorgs, open reqs, teams without a lead. Most people see a gap and wait for someone else to fill it. The move is to be the person it quietly routes through, capture the trail, and walk into calibration with a packet that already did the arguing.

Titles are granted. Evidence is manufactured.

Stop waiting for scope. Go be the gap.

## Caveats

  * The vocabulary here is Google-specific (L4, L5, calibration, packet, TL). The mechanics transfer to any leveled org. The words will not.

  * Manufacturing evidence is not faking it. Every artifact in this piece is real work. The manufacturing is in choosing _where_ to do the work, not in inventing a record.

  * This does not replace hitting your actual bar. You still have to be excellent at your current level. This is what you do on top of that, not instead of it.

  * Timing beats effort here. The same moves in the wrong quarter produce nothing. Watch for the gap first, then move.
