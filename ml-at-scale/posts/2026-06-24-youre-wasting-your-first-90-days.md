spend the first two weeks setting up their dev environment and reading wikis. They sit through five “getting to know you” 1:1s where everyone is vague about what they actually work on.</p><p>They ask their manager for a project that’s “well-scoped for onboarding.”</p><p>They take careful notes.</p><p>They wait.</p><p>Six weeks in, they’ve shipped nothing. Their manager has no strong opinion of them. The interesting projects are already assigned.</p><p>I’ve watched dozens of engineers make this mistake.</p><p>And I’ve figured out what separates the people who look like rockstars by month three from the people who are still “ramping up” at month six.</p><p>It’s not intelligence. It’s not even experience. It’s a mindset shift most people refuse to make.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>The uncomfortable truth about joining a new team</strong></p><p>Here it is:</p><p><em>You just joined. You are a tool to be used. Be serviceable.</em></p><p>I know that sounds harsh. Stick with me.</p><p>When you’re new, you have no credibility, no context, and no leverage. You can’t negotiate for the interesting projects yet. You can’t push back on bad decisions. You can’t say “I’d rather work on X.”</p><p>The fastest path to doing whatever you want is to first prove that you execute. Fast, reliably, on whatever is needed. No ego.</p><p>This is not a permanent state. It’s a sprint that buys you everything that comes after.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>Weeks 1–2: Map people first, systems second</strong></p><p>Your first instinct will be to dive into the codebase. Resist it.</p><p>Start with people. Who are the 20% doing 80% of what matters? Not the most senior by title — the ones whose names come up in every design doc, whose PRs everyone reviews carefully, who get pulled into incidents.</p><p>Find them. Read their PRs. Understand not just <em>what</em> they work on but <em>why</em>. What’s the business problem? What are the tradeoffs they’re navigating?</p><p>Then map the systems. But be ruthless: not everything deserves investigation. Label the non-critical parts as black boxes and move on. If you go down every code path you will never finish onboarding. Use judgment.</p><p>The output of week two: a clear mental model of who matters, which systems matter, and where the gaps are.</p><div><hr></div><p><em>The mindset shift is free. Weeks 3–8 — where engineers either break away from the pack or disappear into the background — is below.</em></p><div class="paywall-jump" data-component-name="PaywallToDOM"></div><div><hr></div><p><strong>Weeks 3–4: Find the thing on fire and instrument it</strong></p><p>There is always a system that nobody fully owns.</p><p>It’s usually doing something important. It probably has sparse documentation and inconsistent monitoring. The people who originally built it have moved on or moved up. Everyone knows it’s a mess but nobody has had the time to fix it.</p><p>This is your entry point.</p><p>Don’t ask for permission. Go understand it. Add observability. Ship something small — a dashboard, a cleanup, a latency fix, anything that makes it visibly better. Tell your team what you did and why it mattered.</p><p>You just became the de facto owner of something real. That’s worth more than six months of clean, well-scoped onboarding tasks.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>Weeks 5–8: Solve your manager’s problem, not yours</strong></p><p>Directly ask your manager: <em>“What’s the biggest thing keeping you up at night right now?”</em></p><p>Then go fix it. Doesn’t matter if it’s unglamorous. Doesn’t matter if it’s not what you’d have chosen. Speed matters more than elegance here.</p><p>This does two things. First, your manager now has concrete evidence you execute under ambiguity. Second, you’ve started building the kind of trust that lets you later say: <em>“I’ve delivered on your priorities. Now here’s what I think we should work on next.”</em></p><p>That’s the trade. You earn influence by being useful first.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>Week 9+: Now you negotiate</strong></p><p>By this point you have something most new hires don’t: a track record.</p><p>You’ve shipped something real. You own something nobody else owns. Your manager has already told someone on a call that you’re “one of the good ones.”</p><p>Now you can ask for the projects you want. Now your opinion in design reviews carries weight. Now when an interesting problem surfaces, you’re one of the names that comes up.</p><p>This is the entire game.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>Why most engineers get this wrong</strong></p><p>They optimize for comfort in the first 90 days instead of impact.</p><p>Reading wikis is comfortable. Careful note-taking is comfortable. Waiting for a well-scoped project is comfortable.</p><p>None of it builds credibility. None of it gives you leverage.</p><p>The engineers I’ve seen grow fastest — at Google and everywhere else — share one trait: they treat the first 90 days as a sprint where the only metric is “did I become someone people want to work with?” Everything else is secondary.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>Caveats</strong></p><p>This playbook is aggressive by design. A few honest notes:</p><ul><li><p><strong>Context matters.</strong> If you’re joining a team with a structured onboarding program and a manager who explicitly wants you to go slow, adapt. The principles still apply but the timeline might stretch.</p></li><li><p><strong>“Tool to be used” is a sprint, not an identity.</strong> Once you have credibility, stop acting like a tool. The goal is agency, not servitude.</p></li><li><p><strong>Not every “thing on fire” is worth owning.</strong> Some systems are abandoned for good reasons. Use judgment before planting your flag.</p></li></ul>
