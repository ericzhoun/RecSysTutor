# How to pick the right ML team

*(and why most people get it wrong) *

y1="3" y2="10"></line><line x1="3" x2="10" y1="21" y2="14"></line></svg></button></div></div></div></a></figure></div><p>I’ve switched ML teams multiple times at Google. Anti-abuse → YouTube Ads → YouTube Shopping Recommendations.</p><p>Each move was deliberate.</p><p>Most people treat team selection like a job application: they try to impress, they take what they get, and they optimize for brand names on a resume.</p><p>That’s backwards.</p><p>You’re choosing where to spend 40+ hours a week, which problems will shape your skills, and which managers will define your trajectory for the next 2-3 years. The stakes are higher than most people treat them.</p><p>Here’s what I’ve learned.</p><p><strong>The fundamental mistake: optimizing for prestige instead of fit</strong></p><p>Everyone wants the “hot” team. The one doing LLMs, the one that shipped the paper everyone’s talking about. It’s understandable. But prestige is a lagging indicator. </p><p>By the time a team is famous, the hard interesting work is often over, and what’s left is maintenance and scale. You’re joining the aftermath, not the frontier.</p><p>The right question isn’t “is this team impressive?” It’s “does this team match where I am <em>right now</em> and where I want to be in 3 years?”</p><p>Those are very different questions.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>What to research before you talk to anyone</strong></p><div class="paywall-jump" data-component-name="PaywallToDOM"></div><p>Before you speak to a single person, do your homework:</p><ul><li><p><strong>What does this team actually ship?</strong> Not the OKRs. The real artifacts. Launched features, model versions, internal tools. If you can’t find evidence of shipping, that’s data.</p></li><li><p><strong>What’s the ML stack?</strong> Some teams run Frankenstein pipelines held together by legacy code. That’s fine if you want infra experience. It’s a trap if you want research depth.</p></li><li><p><strong>What’s the team’s promo history?</strong> Are L4s getting to L5? Or is the ladder stalled because headcount is capped or the team’s impact is hard to quantify? Impact legibility matters for promos.</p></li><li><p><strong>Who left recently, and where did they go?</strong> Attrition patterns tell you more than any team pitch deck.</p></li></ul><div><hr></div><p><strong>The questions you should ask your (future) manager</strong></p><p>The manager conversation is where most people go soft. They want to make a good impression, so they ask safe questions. That’s a mistake. This is your interview of them.</p><ul><li><p><em>“How do you typically give feedback, and how often?”</em> — You want specifics. “I do weekly 1:1s and written mid-year reviews” is an answer. “I have an open door policy, idc” is another.</p></li><li><p><em>“What does success look like for this role in the first 6 months?”</em> — If they can’t answer this concretely, they haven’t thought about your onboarding. You’ll be left to figure it out alone.</p></li><li><p><em>“How do you handle it when a project gets deprioritized or killed?”</em> — Every team has projects that die. What matters is whether the manager protects their people’s impact narrative when that happens, or leaves them holding a dead project at promo time.</p></li><li><p><em>“What’s your relationship with the skip like?”</em> — A manager at war with their skip is a manager who can’t get you resources, headcount, or promo support when you need it.</p></li><li><p><em>“Who on the team is on a strong trajectory right now, and why?”</em> — Listen for whether they talk about outputs and ownership, or whether it’s vague praise. The criteria they use for others are the criteria they’ll use for you.</p></li></ul><div><hr></div><p><strong>The questions you should ask your skip</strong></p><p>Most people never talk to the skip before joining. That’s a missed opportunity — the skip sets the team’s culture ceiling and controls promo calibration above your manager’s level.</p><ul><li><p><em>“What’s the team’s biggest strategic bet in the next 12 months?”</em> — Does it align with what your manager told you? Inconsistency here is a signal.</p></li><li><p><em>“How does this team fit into the org’s broader priorities?”</em> — You want to join a team that’s load-bearing, not one that’s tolerated. Skips will tell you more candidly than managers where the team sits in the pecking order.</p></li><li><p><em>“How do you think about growing senior ICs on this team?”</em> — If you’re L4 targeting L5, you want to hear that the skip has a mental model for what L5 looks like on this team, and that they’ve actually promoted people to it recently.</p></li><li><p><em>“What would make you consider this team a success in 2 years?”</em> — The skip’s answer tells you what will actually get rewarded. If it doesn’t match your interests, that’s important information before you join, not after.</p></li><li><p><em>“Is there anything about this team’s current challenges you’d want me to know going in?”</em> Skips who respect you will answer this honestly. The ones who give you a pure sales pitch are telling you something too.</p></li></ul><div><hr></div><p><strong>The questions you should ask future teammates</strong></p><p>Not the manager. Not the skip. Teammates. They’ll tell you the truth the others won’t.</p><ul><li><p><em>“What did you work on in the last 3 months?”</em> — If the answer is vague or dominated by meetings and planning, that’s a signal.</p></li><li><p><em>“How does ML actually move the needle here? What’s the feedback loop?”</em> — Some teams have tight, measurable loops (ads CTR, rec engagement). Others are in the dark. Know which you’re walking into.</p></li><li><p><em>“What’s the biggest bottleneck right now?”</em> — Is it data? Compute? Unclear strategy? Each answer implies a different day-to-day experience for you.</p></li><li><p><em>“If you could change one thing about the team, what would it be?”</em> — The answer to this question is almost always honest, and almost always important.</p></li><li><p><em>“How does the team treat failure?”</em> — Do experiments that don’t work get buried, or do they get written up and learned from? That’s the difference between a learning culture and a cover-your-ass culture.</p></li></ul><div><hr></div><p><strong>Align the move to your season</strong></p><p>This is the part nobody talks about. The right team depends entirely on where you are in your career and life.</p><p><strong>Early career (L3-L4):</strong> You need mentorship density and tight feedback loops. Pick the team with the best senior engineers who actually review code and care about growth. Don’t pick the team that lets you run alone on hard problems.</p><p><strong>Mid career (L4-L5):</strong> You need ownership and impact legibility. You need problems hard enough that solving them is undeniable on a promo packet. Pick the team where you can point to a system and say “I built that, it moved X metric by Y%.”</p><p><strong>Later stage / post-promo:</strong> You can start optimizing for personal interest and research depth. You’ve earned the credibility to take the longer bet.</p><p>Where you are in <em>life</em> matters too. If you’re in a city for a fixed window, or you have personal goals that require predictable hours, or you’re building something on the side. </p><p>Each one is a different fit.</p><p>A team with brutal crunch cycles is the wrong move no matter how good the ML is. Don’t optimize for resume at the cost of the rest of your life.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>Trust your judgment (especially the uncomfortable signal)</strong></p><p>This is the one I want to leave you with.</p><p>Every team switch I’ve made, there was a moment in the process where something felt off. A vague answer to a direct question. A manager who talked too much about vision and not enough about how the team works day-to-day. </p><p> teammate who seemed tired in a specific way.</p><p>I ignored those signals once. I shouldn’t have.</p><p>Your judgment is running a pattern-matching model trained on everything you’ve experienced in your career. When it fires an uncomfortable signal, that’s not anxiety, that’s data. Don’t override it with prestige logic or with the sunk cost of having already started the process.</p><p>The best team switch I made was the one where I trusted a quiet “this is right” before I had all the facts. The worst was the one where I talked myself out of a quiet “something’s off” because the project looked good on paper.</p><p>The model in your gut is usually right. Trust it.</p><div><hr></div><p><strong>The short version</strong></p><ul><li><p>Research before you talk to anyone</p></li><li><p>Ask the manager, then the skip, then teammates — in that order</p></li><li><p>Match the team to your career season and your life season</p></li><li><p>The uncomfortable signal is data, not anxiety</p></li><li><p>Prestige is a lagging indicator — fit is what compounds</p></li></ul><p>— Ludo</p>
