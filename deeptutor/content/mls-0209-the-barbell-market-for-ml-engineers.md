years than in the previous decade combined.</p><p>And it’s creating two very different winners while quietly eliminating everyone in between.</p><p>I call it the barbell market. Heavy on both ends. Nothing in the middle.</p><div><hr></div><h2>The Barbell</h2><p>In finance, a barbell strategy means you put weight at the extremes and avoid the middle entirely. Maximum risk on one end, maximum safety on the other, nothing in the comfortable center that feels safe but isn’t.</p><p>That’s exactly what’s happening to ML engineering right now.</p><p>On one end of the barbell: the people building foundation models.</p><p>On the other: the AI engineers shipping products on top of them.</p><p>Both ends are thriving. Both ends have real demand, real leverage, real futures.</p><p>The middle, where most ML engineers actually live, is the bar itself. And it’s getting crushed.</p><div><hr></div><h2>One End: The Foundation Model Engineers</h2><p>At one extreme, you have the engineers and researchers building the actual models. </p><p>The LLMs. </p><p>The generative recommendation systems.</p><p>The E2E architectures replacing stacks that took entire teams years to assemble.</p><p>These are the people working on pre-training runs, RLHF pipelines, model architecture at a scale where the problems are genuinely unsolved.</p><p>They’re not applying models. They’re building the substrate everyone else builds on top of.</p><p>This group is thriving. Demand is extraordinary, compensation reflects it, and the work is getting <em>more</em> technically rich, not less.</p><p>The better foundation models get, the more critical it becomes to have people who can push them further. Paradoxically, the AI boom hasn’t commoditized this work: it made it more valuable.</p><p>The engineers closest to core model work — training infrastructure, large-scale optimization, architecture research — exist in a completely different labor market than everyone else. The gap widens every quarter.</p><p>The catch: this end of the barbell is almost impossible to enter mid-career. You don’t get here with hustle and tutorials. The barrier is genuine depth in a narrow area most people never develop.</p><p>The weight is heavy because the bar to lift it is real.</p><div><hr></div><p><em>I write about ML systems in production — the tradeoffs, the architecture decisions, the stuff that doesn’t make it into papers. If you want to go deeper, the paid tier covers the technical details I can’t fit in free posts.</em></p><h2>The Other End: The AI Engineers</h2><div class="paywall-jump" data-component-name="PaywallToDOM"></div><p>At the opposite extreme, you have the AI engineer. They chain API calls, write prompts, integrate LLM outputs into product surfaces, and ship things users actually see.</p><p>They move fast and they’re genuinely valuable.</p><p>Especially to startups that need to build quickly without a research team behind them.</p><p>This end of the barbell is accessible. You can get here in months. The tooling keeps improving, the abstractions keep rising, and a skilled AI engineer can produce real leverage for a business without needing to understand what’s happening two layers below.</p><p>The ceiling, though, is visible from day one. When your core skill is orchestrating models other people built, your leverage depends entirely on that orchestration remaining complex enough to require skill. And the trajectory of tooling is to make it easier, not harder. What requires craft today requires a template tomorrow.</p><p>That’s not fatal.</p><p>Domain expertise, taste, and speed matter a lot at this end.</p><p>But the moat is shallow and the end is crowded. The weight here is light but accessible. A lot of people can pick it up.</p><div><hr></div><h2>The Middle: Where Most MLEs Actually Live</h2><p>The most disrupted group isn’t the AI engineer. It’s the traditional senior MLE. The person who spent years building feature pipelines, owning recommendation stacks, tuning ranking models, shipping production ML systems with real engineering rigor. That person that is competent, experienced, well-compensated is in a genuinely difficult position.</p><p>Their work isn’t disappearing overnight. But the trajectory is clear.</p><p>Foundation models are collapsing workflows that used to require entire specialized teams.</p><p>E2E approaches are eating the modular stacks that senior MLEs spent careers mastering.</p><p>And the specific technical knowledge that made someone valuable in 2019 is depreciating faster than anyone wants to admit.</p><p>I’ve felt this personally.</p><p>I’ve watched systems that once required deep specialized expertise get thinner and thinner as the underlying models get smarter. The work shifts in ways that don’t always favor the people who were best at the old version of it.</p><p>The brutal business logic is simple: why pay for someone expensive who sits in the middle when you can hire a cheap AI engineer for the surface layer and a brilliant researcher for the depth and get more leverage from both?</p><p>The middle isn’t just technically squeezed. It’s economically squeezed.</p><p>This is the bar of the barbell.</p><p>It’s where the weight lands. And most MLEs are standing right under it.</p><div><hr></div><h2>What Do You Do With This?</h2><p>The naive answer is “learn more AI.” Fine-tune something. Add LLMs to your resume. That gets you employed, but it doesn’t answer the harder question: which end of the barbell are you actually moving toward?</p><p>Because that’s the real decision now. And not making it is itself a decision — to stay in the middle and hope it holds.</p><p>If you’re moving toward foundational depth, go all the way. Get closer to the models, to the research, to the genuinely unsolved problems. It’s slow and hard and the entry bar is brutal. But it leads somewhere defensible.</p><p>If you’re moving toward the AI engineer end, then stop pretending you’re competing on depth and start building a different kind of moat: domain expertise, speed, distribution, the ability to translate between what models can do and what businesses actually need. That’s real leverage. But it requires being honest about what game you’re playing.</p><p>The worst position is the comfortable middle. Knowing enough to feel legitimate. Not deep enough to be irreplaceable. Expensive enough to be a line item someone eventually questions.</p><div><hr></div><h2>A Personal Note</h2><p>Four years inside Google’s ML infrastructure gives you a view that’s hard to get elsewhere. I’ve built systems at a scale where the engineering problems are genuinely interesting and I’ve watched those same systems evolve in ways that would have seemed impossible a few years ago.</p><p>My honest read: the engineers who come through this well are the ones being ruthlessly honest about where they actually sit on the barbell. Not where they want to be. Not where their resume says they are. Where the <em>work</em> is pulling them.</p><p>The barbell is real. Both ends are good places to be. The middle is not.</p><p>The only question is which end you’re picking up and whether you’re making that choice deliberately, or just hoping the bar doesn’t fall.</p><div><hr></div><p><em>Where do you see yourself on the barbell?
