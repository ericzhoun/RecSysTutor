# How You Actually Grow as an MLE 

*Machine Learning at Scale collection — Ludovico Bessi, 2026-03-04 · topic: career*

[](../assets/26a0afd9ad8561a4.png)

Most people think levelling up is about getting better at coding.

It’s not. Or at least, that’s not the interesting part.

Here’s how I actually think about the levels and what changes at each one.

**L3: You execute**

Someone tells you what to do and you do it well. Try this experiment, model it after this existing one, here’s the design: go implement it.

Blocked? You flag it immediately and ask your tech lead.

Your job is precise execution and clear communication about where you’re stuck. You’re not expected to figure out what to build. You’re expected to build it correctly once someone tells you.

That’s not a criticism. It’s the right way to start. You’re learning the system, the codebase, the culture, how decisions get made. Absorb everything.

**L4: You operate independently**

No more hand-holding. You are the team’s coding horsepower.

You pick up tasks without being told how to approach them. You start writing design docs, maybe with some support from a senior engineer the first few times. You’re not just flagging problems anymore. You’re showing up with proposed solutions.

This is where most ML engineers spend a significant chunk of their career. And honestly? Being a great L4 is genuinely valuable.

Teams need people who can just execute at high velocity without creating overhead for everyone around them.

What comes after L4 is where it gets interesting and where most people get it wrong.

**The levels, the paths, how I personally think about my own trajectory, and the archetype that’s worked for me.**

**L5: You design and you guide**

This is where the job fundamentally changes.

You’re not just building things anymore. You’re deciding what to build.

You take a large problem, break it into components, and figure out who does what.

You start guiding L3s, helping them grow without doing the work for them.

The evaluation criteria shifts too. At L4 you’re measured on what you shipped.

At L5 you’re increasingly measured on impact — did the thing you decided to build actually move the needle? The accountability goes up.

You become the go-to expert for a part of the system. People come to you with questions not because you’re senior in title but because you know that area better than anyone.

L5 is also where the path forks. You can go deeper as an IC — Staff, Principal — or start thinking about the management track. Both are legitimate. Neither is the default right answer.

**L6: You set direction**

You’re writing roadmaps. Where should this team go in the next 6 months, the next year?

You start owning team-level metrics, not just project-level outcomes.

Some people at this level start taking on management responsibilities.

Others stay deeply technical but operate at a scope that affects the entire team’s trajectory.

**What nobody tells you about leveling up**

Technical ability is table stakes at every level. Yes, you need to be good. But being good at coding is not what gets you promoted past L4.

What actually matters — at every level, more than anything else — is visibility, ownership, and impact.

**Visibility** : are the right people aware of what you’re doing and why it matters? This isn’t about self-promotion. It’s about making sure your work connects to things the organization cares about.

**Ownership** : do you treat the system like it’s yours?

Not just your piece of it. The whole thing.

If something is broken and it’s not technically your problem, do you fix it anyway?

The engineers who grow fastest are the ones who are always asking “what’s the most pressing issue right now” and going to fix it, regardless of whose name is on the ticket.

**Impact** : at the end of the day this is all that matters.

Did you move metrics? Did your ideas pay off? Did the things you built make the system meaningfully better?

**My personal archetype**

I am, at heart, a coder. I just do a lot of things.

20 experiments running in parallel. High velocity, high volume.

My theory is simple: if I run enough good experiments, impact becomes inevitable. Some will fail. Enough will work.

I’ve also started scaling myself.

Simple experiments I’m confident will pay off I hand those to new joiners as onboarding projects.

They learn the system, the experiment gets done, I free up bandwidth for harder problems. Everyone wins.

Where do I want to go from here? I want to stay an IC.

I want to own bigger and bigger parts of the system, drive technical direction, and be the person teams come to when they have a hard problem.

Not because of my title but because of what I know and what I’ve shipped.
