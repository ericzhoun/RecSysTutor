# On AI Psychosis

*Machine Learning at Scale collection — Ludovico Bessi, 2026-09-27 · topic: meta*

*my take and what I am changing at work to keep up :)*

[](../assets/72c60972013bbf9b.jpg)

I know exactly what happens if I walk back to my desk after a 4pm meeting.

One last PR. The agent is already halfway through something, so I review it, and while I’m there I notice another thing, and I kick that off too. One becomes ten. Ten becomes 7pm. Nobody asked me to do any of it.

No launch, no deadline, no manager pinging me.

My AI psychosis is the feeling that every idle minute is a minute some agent could have been shipping for me.

I don’t think I’m the only one. Let’s talk about it!

### The filter broke

Starting things used to be expensive. An idea on the tram needed a weekend.

An experiment needed a day of plumbing before it produced a single number.

A side project needed a month before it did anything you could show someone.

So I always had more ideas than hours, and the hours did the filtering for me.

Most ideas died quietly because they weren’t worth a weekend. I never had to decide they weren’t worth it. The cost decided.

But now?

Side project idea at 7pm → prototype by 10pm.  
Hunch about a ranking feature → three experiments before lunch.  
One PR merged → the agent already has the next one drafted.

At work there’s always another PR I could be sending.

At home there’s always another side project I could be spinning up.

In between there’s always one more experiment worth launching.

Every one more thing is cheap. That’s the trap.

Nothing individually is expensive enough to say no to, so I end up saying yes to all of it.

### It feels great, which is the problem

The first months of this were the most fun I’ve had engineering in years. Things I’d been sitting on for a long time suddenly got built. Ideas I’d written off became weekend projects, then evening projects, then lunch break projects.

And the loop is sticky in a very specific way. You fire off a task, you go do something else, you come back and something happened.

Sometimes it’s great. Sometimes it’s garbage.

You never know which, so you keep checking. I am refreshing my agent’s output like it’s a feed.

Everyone around you is running more agents, shipping more PRs, launching more experiments. If you stop, you feel like you’re falling behind. So you don’t stop.

The work itself wasn’t even hard. I just never decided I was done.

### The bottleneck moved

For most of my career the scarce thing was execution. Can I build it, can I build it fast enough, can I get it through review.

That constraint mostly dissolved. What’s scarce now is judgment about what deserves building at all, and attention to actually look at what came back.

Ten half-reviewed experiments are worth less than three I actually understood.

I know that. I still catch myself launching the ten.

**Two things I do now**

**I decide before the inbox does.** Every morning, before email, before chat, before the notifications, I write a short list of what I think is most worth doing today. What I would work on if nobody sent me anything.

The list isn’t sacred. Priorities change, prod breaks, someone needs something urgently, and I adjust. Fine.

But I adjust from a position I picked. If I skip this, the first twenty pings of the day pick for me, and by 11am I’ve done a lot of things and none of them were mine.

It takes five minutes. It’s the highest leverage five minutes of my day, because it’s the only point where I’m deciding before anything is competing for the decision.

**I bring the gym bag to the meeting.** If I plan to train after a 4pm meeting, the bag comes into the meeting room with me, and I walk straight out of the building afterwards.

I don’t win willpower fights against an open laptop with an agent halfway through a task. So I stopped having them. I take the desk out of the path.

Both habits are the same trick. Make the decision when you’re calm, then make it cheaper to keep than to break. The version of me at 8am, with coffee and no notifications, is a much better judge of how my day should go than the version of me at 5:45pm with a half-finished diff on screen.

So I let the 8am version set things up, and I make it hard for the 5:45pm version to undo it.

**None of this means doing less**

I want to be careful here, because this could easily read as “slow down, touch grass.”

Some days the right call really is ten experiments in parallel and babysitting all of them until late. Some weekends the right call is building the side project, because I’m excited about it and it’s the best use of that time. I’ve had those days and I’ll have more.

What changed is that I pick them. At 8am, with a clear head, knowing what I’m trading away. The ten experiments are a choice I made, and I made room for them. They didn’t just happen to me because the queue was there and I was near it.

Same amount of work, possibly. Very different relationship with it.

**The job now**

The tools will keep getting faster. The queue won’t get shorter. There will never be a day where I look at the backlog and there’s nothing left worth doing, because the backlog now grows at the speed of my ideas instead of the speed of my typing.

Deciding what’s worth doing used to come free with being slow.

Now it’s the job!
