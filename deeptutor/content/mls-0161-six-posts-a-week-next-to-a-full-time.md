The second most common question in my DMs. (what’s the first one? Comment below!)

How do you post six times a week on LinkedIn, run a newsletter, and hold down a demanding job? I can’t even manage one post a month.

The answer is… OPERATIONS!

But I want to answer it properly, because the assumed answer is wrong.

People think the answer is discipline, or passion, or that I type fast.

Some suspect the answer is that I’m secretly bad at my job. :)

The actual answer is that I stopped treating content as a daily creative act and built a machine. The machine has parts. Let’s check them out!

None of them require talent, and I can prove that to you!

### The real bottleneck

People who can’t post consistently always diagnose themselves the same way: no time, no ideas.

Both diagnoses are wrong, and you can verify it yourself.

You have twenty minutes today; a post takes twenty minutes to write. And you had at least three postable thoughts this week: something at work surprised you, annoyed you, or taught you something. Time and ideas exist.

The actual bottleneck is that you’re running every post through a decision gauntlet: what should I write about, is this idea good enough, what will colleagues think, is this the right day, should I rewrite it again.

Ten decisions per post, ofc you don’t write anything!

The machine’s entire purpose is removing decisions, not adding effort.

## The machine, overview

→ Every scar, surprise, and rant from the day job goes into one running note, in the moment, unfiltered. The note is always full. “No ideas” died the week this habit started.

→ Writing is batched. Posts are written in one sessions per week, not daily.  
Daily writing means paying the startup cost of “getting into writing mode” six times. Batching pays it once. This single change roughly x2 my output.

→ Technical or career, defined formats within each. A captured idea sorts itself into a lane and a format in seconds. “What should I write about” is already decided!

→ Fixed slots, scheduled in advance. Zero day-of decisions. The post goes out whether I feel like a thought leader that morning or not, which is good, because I mostly don’t :D

## The part people don’t believe

The machine takes me 2 hours a week. All of them next to a full-time job I’m actively trying to get promoted in.

The two don’t compete for time nearly as much as people assume, and the reason why is the actual relationship between the day job and the content. let’s find out:

  1. the full weekly schedule with real hours

  2. the capture-to-published pipeline in detail

  3. the format templates that make writing fast, the day-job relationship that makes the whole thing sustainable, and the three rules that prevent the machine from eating the life it’s supposed to fund.

Copy the machine as described and you’ll publish more this month than last quarter, on hours you already have ;)

**1\. The content doesn’t compete with the day job, it is fueled by it :D**  
  
Every post is a byproduct of work I was doing anyway: the scar becomes the confessional, the debug becomes the teardown, the launch becomes the lesson.  
  
People burn out because they run two separate production lines: job over here, “content creation” over there, each demanding its own raw material.  
I run one line with two outputs.

This is also why the content got better when the job got harder, which is the opposite of what everyone predicts.

2\. **The weekly schedule, real:**

Post in the free time. Comment in the free time. That works. But the actual content? 

Take every week 1h of FOCUS TIME and write as much as possible, just whatever you are thinking / jotted down in the notes in the week. Total honest hours? 2h / week.

**3\. Format templates:** pick some formats / topics you like talking about. Choose a % of them and get on with it. As an example, mine are:

A) Deep tech content around scaling ML systems

B) Living in Zurich vs other European cities

C) Other random misc content

Never start from a blank page!

**4\. The three sustainability rules** : 

The machine runs at a pace my worst week can sustain, not my best week. I schedule content IN ADVANCE. a lot of it. I always have a month of content scheduled at least because i need time to make good content and I don’t want to rush it.

**5\. What I’d tell month-one me** : Don’t be scared of low engagement in the beginning. Have a controversial opinion that other people engage with and push that hard!

**6\. Boundary note:** remain focused on what actually matters. for me it’s a strong performance at my day job AND i want to keep my day job. that means no stupid comments on internal things, EVER!
